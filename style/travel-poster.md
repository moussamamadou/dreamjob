# Travel Poster: style guide

A portable visual style for establishing shots, journeys, eras and places, in video, thumbnails, covers and channel art. It works in any project: swap the palette, keep the rules.

**In one line:** every scene is a mid-century screen-printed travel poster: big places, big skies, machines on the move, and people so small they never have faces.

**Why it is face-free:** the place is the subject. People appear only at poster scale: tiny figures on a platform, a crew on a deck, a car on a road. At that size nothing has a face to drift, and the place itself (Chicago, the Caribbean, the Badlands) is easy to keep consistent.

**Where it comes from:** 1930s to 1950s railway, airline and national-park posters, US WPA screen-print posters, and the flat landscapes of 1950s animation backgrounds (Amid Amidi, *Cartoon Modern*, Chronicle Books, 2006). *References from memory: check before quoting them on screen.*

---

## 1. Core rules

1. **The place is the hero.** One location per frame, shown big: skyline, harbour, desert, prison island.
2. **People are under 5% of frame height.** No exceptions. If a person must be bigger, use another style.
3. **Machines carry the story.** Trains, ships, cars, planes, trucks: they show movement, money and era.
4. **Screen-print look.** Four to five flat inks, slight mis-registration, coarse grain, no gradients except stepped sky bands.
5. **Strong diagonal.** A road, rail, coastline or wake cuts across the frame.
6. **Period-correct, always.** Only buildings, vehicles and dress that existed in that year. Image models add modern landmarks; check every frame.
7. **One accent:** a signal light, a flag, a red car.

## 2. Palette

| Role | Share | Default (Dream Job) |
|---|---|---|
| Sky and paper | 30 to 50% | cream `#EFE3C8`, stepped mustard bands at dusk |
| Water and shade | 20 to 35% | teal `#2F7C78` |
| Land and buildings | 15 to 30% | ink `#24211D`, mustard `#D9A23A` |
| Accent | under 3% | red `#C23A2B` |

## 3. Composition and type

- Horizon low (a big sky) or high (a big landscape); never in the middle.
- Leave a clean sky band for the title: travel posters always had one. Add the text in Remotion, in a slab or a condensed grotesk in caps.
- Use it at the start of a lesson or a move to a new city ("Lesson 2: Onboarding. Chicago, 1920").

## 4. Motion

- **In MiniMax: excellent.** Clouds drift, smoke trails, water ripples, a train or ship crosses frame. One motion per clip; slow camera push or pan.
- Prompt: "the steam train slowly crosses the bridge from left to right, smoke drifting, clouds move slowly, camera static".
- QA: buildings that appear or melt, vehicles that duplicate, modern skylines creeping in.

## 5. Prompt template

```
1950s travel poster illustration, flat screenprint style with off-register colour and coarse grain. [Place] in [year], [time of day], [machine] [doing what] in the foreground. Tiny distant figures too small to have faces. Bold simplified shapes, strong diagonal composition, large sky. Only buildings and vehicles that existed in [year]. Palette only: cream #EFE3C8, mustard #D9A23A, teal #2F7C78, ink #24211D, one small red #C23A2B [accent]. No text, no letters.
```

**Warning from the sample:** the test frame of "Chicago" drew the Willis Tower (finished 1973) in a 1920s scene. Always name the year and the landmarks of that year, or describe a generic skyline.

## 6. When to use it

**Strong for:** establishing shots, journeys, a new city or ship, era changes, chapter openers, channel banners, book back covers. Reusable for any history, travel or geography channel.

**Weak for:** anything a person does up close. Pair it with a character style (Wooden Manikin, Turned Away, Cut Paper Noir).

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] People are tiny and faceless.
- [ ] Every building and vehicle fits the year.
- [ ] Four or five flat inks, one accent.
- [ ] A clean band for the title.
- [ ] No text in the art.
